Overview

The Caribbean Import Landscape

The Caribbean basin represents one of the world's most dynamic commodity import markets. Island and coastal economies across the region — from Jamaica and the Dominican Republic to Trinidad and Tobago, the Bahamas, Haiti, and Guyana — depend almost entirely on imported fuel and food to sustain daily commerce, power generation, transportation, and basic nutrition.

For fuel importers, this means a constant demand cycle for ultra-low sulfur diesel (ULSD), gasoline, Jet A-1 aviation fuel, LPG, heavy fuel oil, and marine gas oil. Power utilities, fuel distributors, airport operators, and fleet owners require reliable supply at predictable landed costs. A single disruption in the supply chain — a missed vessel, a documentation error, a financing delay — can have cascading effects on an entire market.

Food commodity importers face a similar reality. Staple products including rice, sugar, wheat flour, cooking oil, legumes, frozen poultry, and pork are sourced overwhelmingly from external producers. The U.S. Gulf Coast is the primary origin for many of these goods, offering proximity, quality certification, and established shipping lanes to Caribbean ports.

This playbook is designed for importers, distributors, fuel terminal operators, government procurement agencies, and food wholesale buyers who need to understand the mechanics of CIF delivery into Caribbean markets. It covers the full transaction lifecycle: how pricing works, what logistics look like, which ports receive what cargo, and how trade finance can enable transactions that would otherwise stall.

Who This Applies To

  • Fuel terminal operators and petroleum distributors in Jamaica, Dominican Republic, and the wider Caribbean
  • Food importers and wholesale distributors sourcing rice, poultry, sugar, and cooking oil
  • Government procurement agencies managing bulk commodity purchases
  • Port-side businesses requiring regular CIF delivery of US-origin products
  • New market entrants evaluating Caribbean commodity supply chains
Delivery Structure

CIF vs FOB: How Caribbean Delivery Works

The distinction between CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight) and FOB (Free on Board) is fundamental to how Caribbean commodity imports are structured. For most importers in the region, CIF is the preferred Incoterm — and for good reason.

What CIF Means in Practice

Under a CIF delivery structure, the supplier assumes responsibility for three critical cost components: the product itself, marine cargo insurance covering the full shipment value, and ocean freight from the load port to the named destination port. The buyer receives a single landed price — one number that covers everything up to the ship's arrival at their terminal or port.

For a fuel importer in Kingston, Jamaica, this means the quoted CIF price includes the cost of ULSD loaded at a U.S. Gulf Coast terminal, insurance covering the full cargo during transit, and the vessel charter or freight allocation to deliver the product to Kingston Harbour. There are no surprise freight invoices, no separate insurance policies to arrange, and no coordination with multiple shipping counterparties.

Why CIF Dominates Caribbean Trade

Caribbean importers overwhelmingly prefer CIF for several reasons. First, most buyers lack the scale or infrastructure to efficiently arrange ocean freight independently. Chartering a tanker or booking container slots requires established relationships with shipowners, brokers, and P&I clubs that smaller importers simply do not have. Second, CIF simplifies the financial exposure — the buyer knows their total cost before the vessel loads. Third, CIF shifts transit risk to the supplier's insurance policy, reducing the buyer's liability during the ocean voyage.

For food commodities, CIF delivery is equally standard. A rice importer in Santo Domingo or a poultry distributor in Port-au-Prince receives product at their named port with freight and insurance fully covered. This allows importers to calculate their landed cost, apply their margin, and price for the local market with certainty.

FOB: When It Makes Sense

FOB terms — where the buyer takes ownership at the load port — are occasionally used by larger Caribbean importers who operate their own shipping logistics or have existing freight agreements. Government tenders sometimes specify FOB pricing to allow the state's own maritime department to arrange transport. However, for the majority of commercial importers in the region, CIF remains the standard.

Pricing

What Drives Landed Cost

Understanding how Caribbean commodity pricing works is essential for any importer looking to manage costs effectively. Whether you are importing ULSD diesel into Kingston, gasoline into Caucedo, or rice into Port-au-Prince, the same fundamental pricing components apply.

Fuel Pricing Components

The landed cost of fuel in Caribbean markets is built from several layers. The base product cost reflects the prevailing Gulf Coast benchmark — Platts USGC for diesel products, RBOB for gasoline, or the applicable index for jet fuel. On top of this base, the supplier adds a differential that accounts for product quality specifications, minimum order quantities, and prevailing supply-demand conditions.

Freight cost is the second major component. Transit from the U.S. Gulf Coast to Jamaica typically takes 3–5 days, to the Dominican Republic 4–6 days, and to more distant Caribbean destinations 5–8 days. Freight rates fluctuate with vessel availability, bunker costs, and seasonal shipping patterns. A well-connected supplier with established vessel relationships can meaningfully reduce freight exposure for the buyer.

Marine cargo insurance, port charges, inspection fees (typically SGS or equivalent), and documentation costs round out the total CIF price. For fuel shipments, the inspection component is particularly important — independent quantity and quality verification at the load port protects both parties and is standard practice in the industry.

Food Commodity Pricing

Food commodities follow a similar structure, though with different benchmarks. Rice pricing typically references USDA-reported values and seasonal harvest cycles. Poultry pricing tracks USDA leg quarter indices. Sugar, cooking oil, and wheat flour each have their own reference markets. The freight component for containerized food shipments differs from bulk tanker rates, and port handling charges vary by commodity type and destination infrastructure.

Managing Cost Volatility

  • Lock pricing against a benchmark at a specific date to reduce index exposure
  • Secure freight rates in advance through established shipping relationships
  • Bundle multiple commodity shipments to optimize vessel utilization
  • Establish recurring supply agreements with volume-based pricing tiers
  • Work with a capital-backed supplier who can absorb short-term market swings
Logistics & Ports

Shipping Routes & Caribbean Ports

The logistics of delivering fuel and food to Caribbean markets are shaped by geography, port infrastructure, and the specific requirements of each commodity. The U.S. Gulf Coast — Houston, New Orleans, and surrounding terminals — serves as the primary origin point, offering the shortest transit times and the most competitive freight rates to the Caribbean basin.

Key Destination Ports

Port of Kingston
Jamaica

The largest natural harbour in the Caribbean. Kingston serves as Jamaica's primary fuel and food import gateway, with dedicated petroleum storage terminals, container handling facilities, and bulk cargo berths. Transit from Houston: 3–5 days.

Port of Caucedo
Dominican Republic

A modern deep-water multipurpose port near Santo Domingo. Caucedo handles containerized food commodities, petroleum products, and general cargo. The adjacent free trade zone supports distribution across the Dominican Republic. Transit from Houston: 4–6 days.

Port of Mariel
Cuba

Cuba's principal commercial port west of Havana, developed as a special economic zone. Mariel handles bulk cargo and containerized goods, serving as the country's primary gateway for food and commodity imports. Transit from Houston: 2–3 days.

Shipping Routes and Vessel Types

Fuel shipments from the U.S. Gulf Coast to Caribbean terminals typically move on clean petroleum tankers ranging from 5,000 to 40,000 DWT, depending on volume and port draft limitations. Major fuel terminals in Kingston and Caucedo can accommodate larger tankers, while smaller Caribbean destinations may require coastal tankers or barge deliveries.

Food commodities generally move in 20-foot and 40-foot containers on regular liner services operating between Gulf Coast ports and Caribbean destinations. Reefer containers are used for frozen poultry and temperature-sensitive products. Bulk dry cargo — rice in large volumes, for example — may move on handysize or supramax bulk carriers when quantities justify it.

The Gulf Coast-to-Caribbean corridor benefits from frequent vessel schedules, competitive rates due to short distances, and well-established documentation protocols that facilitate customs clearance at destination ports.

Trade Finance

How Trade Finance Enables Transactions

Trade finance is not merely a support function in Caribbean commodity imports — it is often the decisive factor that determines whether a transaction occurs at all. Many importers in the region have strong local demand, established distribution networks, and solid payment histories, but lack the upfront capital or international credit facilities needed to secure bulk commodity shipments from U.S. suppliers.

When Financing Is Used

Trade finance bridges the gap between a supplier who requires payment before or at the time of loading and a buyer who generates revenue only after the product arrives and is distributed. This gap — often 30 to 90 days — is the fundamental problem that trade finance solves.

In practice, this means a diesel distributor in Jamaica can receive a CIF cargo of ULSD without paying the full purchase price upfront. Instead, the financing structure allows the importer to take delivery, sell the product through their distribution network, and remit payment within an agreed term. For food importers purchasing containerized shipments of rice or poultry, the same principle applies — the financing enables the physical transaction.

Who Qualifies

  • Established importers with documented trade history and verifiable receivables
  • Companies with existing distribution infrastructure (terminals, warehouses, retail networks)
  • Buyers with consistent demand patterns and predictable revenue cycles
  • Government agencies and state-owned enterprises with sovereign backing
  • New importers who can demonstrate market access and provide appropriate security

Why It Matters

Without access to trade finance, many Caribbean importers are limited to spot purchases at quantities far below their actual market demand. A distributor who could move 5,000 MT of diesel per month may only be able to finance 1,000 MT at a time, losing market share and paying higher per-unit costs. Integrated trade finance — where the commodity supplier and the financing counterparty are the same entity — eliminates the friction of coordinating between separate banks, traders, and logistics providers. The transaction moves faster, documentation is simpler, and the total cost of the deal is often lower.
